Can a Certain Sound Frequency or “Hz” Video Cure Tinnitus?

Tinnitus Claims: Fact vs. Evidence

! Misleading Evidence verdict

Sound can give real, temporary relief. Nothing in the research shows that a particular frequency or a video permanently cures tinnitus.

The claim

“A specific frequency, such as 432 Hz, 528 Hz or a binaural-beat track, can retrain your brain and cure your tinnitus, and a free video can do it.”

The short answer

Sound can change how tinnitus feels while you listen. That part is real, and it is why sound is used in tinnitus clinics. What is not supported is the idea that one “healing” frequency, or a video, removes tinnitus for good.

  • 432 Hz and 528 Hz: we found no published human tinnitus-treatment trials specifically evaluating these frequencies (PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov searches, 15 October 2026).
  • Binaural beats: two small studies found no clear advantage over the same sound without the beats.[1, 2]
  • Notched (“gap”) music: an early small study was positive, but larger controlled trials mostly did not find it better than ordinary music.[3, 4, 5, 6]
  • Masking and sound enrichment: regulators accept sound devices for temporary relief, and guidelines disagree about longer-term value.[7, 8, 9, 10, 11]

“Unsupported” here does not mean every sound has been shown not to work. For 432 Hz and 528 Hz, it means we found no published human trials of them for tinnitus, not that they have been shown not to work.

What the research shows

What “Hz” claims are really claiming

Hertz (Hz) is the pitch of a tone. Tinnitus pitch differs from person to person, and we found no research linking a tone chosen for its supposed “healing” properties to a person’s tinnitus. The 432 Hz and 528 Hz claims we found in videos rest on assertion, not on tinnitus research: searches of PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov on 15 October 2026 for tinnitus with either number found no human tinnitus-treatment trial specifically evaluating either frequency (the few PubMed hits were unrelated papers).

Clinical sound research uses frequencies matched to a person’s own tinnitus or hearing loss, tested against a comparison sound. That is a different thing from a single “universal” frequency.

What sound can do: masking and temporary relief

A tinnitus masker is a regulated medical device. U.S. regulations describe it as a device “intended to generate noise of sufficient intensity and bandwidth to mask ringing in the ears”, in Class II.[7] Cleared sound devices are indicated for the temporary relief of tinnitus symptoms, for use as part of a management programme with a hearing professional.[8, 12]

Some people also get a short-lived drop in tinnitus loudness after certain sounds. In one laboratory study of 20 people with chronic tinnitus, at least one of 17 sounds reduced loudness in 90% of them, most often amplitude-modulated tones near the tinnitus pitch. The authors described this as temporary and said the long-term effects remained to be investigated.[13]

Does long-term listening help? Reviews and guidelines disagree

Cochrane reviewers looked for controlled trials of sound devices. In 2012 they found six trials (553 people) and no significant change in tinnitus loudness or overall severity compared with other approaches, adding that the absence of conclusive evidence “should not be interpreted as evidence of lack of effectiveness”.[14] A 2018 Cochrane review of hearing aids and sound generators found eight studies (590 people) and no data on the main comparisons against waiting list, placebo or information only.[15]

Guidelines read the same gap differently:

  • The 2026 U.S. guideline says clinicians may recommend sound enrichment (an option, not a firm recommendation).[9]
  • The 2019 European guideline says sound therapy “may be useful for acute relief purposes but is not considered an effective intervention with long-term results”.[16]
  • The German guideline says sound therapy “should not be practiced”, noting that comparison conditions are often missing and changes may reflect normal habituation.[10]
  • The UK’s NICE guideline made no recommendation on sound therapy and called for research.[11]

Taken together: sound is a reasonable comfort tool for many people, and we found no study showing that it is a cure.

Binaural beats

A binaural beat is an illusion: two slightly different tones, one in each ear, make you hear a pulsing third tone. Two small studies tested it. In 20 adults, an 8 Hz binaural beat added to ocean sound showed no significant group benefit beyond the ocean sound alone.[1] In a trial of 30 men, listening for 15 minutes twice a day for four weeks, both the music-only group and the music-plus-beats group improved on tinnitus questionnaires, and the groups did not differ on nearly every measure; the authors wrote that the study “failed to demonstrate the superiority” of adding beats.[2]

That both groups improved is the point: relaxing sound, time and expectation can all lower how much tinnitus bothers a person, which is why a comparison group is essential.

Notched music and “brain retraining” sounds

Notched music removes a band of frequencies around the tinnitus pitch, on the theory that the brain’s overactive area calms down. The first small controlled study (16 people, 12 months of listening) reported reduced loudness and reduced auditory-cortex activity.[3] Later trials were less encouraging. In a double-blind trial of 100 people, 3 months of listening did not change the main outcomes.[4] A trial of 104 people found no significant difference in handicap scores against ordinary music.[5] A 2024 meta-analysis of three studies (99 notched-music and 109 comparison participants) found no significant difference in distress against listening to music, with the authors calling for more research.[6] The German guideline recommends against it.[10]

Newer designs are still being tested. In a 2025 blinded online crossover trial, 53 people who completed it reported lower tinnitus loudness after a specially modulated sound but not after a near-identical sham sound, while distress fell in both periods; the authors list further optimisation as the next step.[17] That is an encouraging early result from one study, not an established treatment.

Why a video can feel like it works

Tinnitus naturally rises and falls. Many people also feel better simply from relaxing, from sleeping better, or from expecting relief. In the binaural-beat trial above and in the 2025 online trial, distress improved in the comparison condition too. A video that makes you feel calmer for an hour has done something real, but it has not shown that it changed the underlying tinnitus.

Limitations and conflicts of interest

Sound studies are mostly small and short, use different questionnaires and comparison sounds, and are hard to blind (you can tell what you are hearing). Several programmes were designed by the people who tested them or by companies that sell them, so funding and independence matter; each treatment page on this site rates them. Most “Hz” claims have no study behind them at all, so there is nothing to appraise. One 2025 trial received a different participant count in press coverage than in its published abstract; we use the abstract.

What you can do

  • If tinnitus is new, in one ear, pulsing, or comes with hearing loss or dizziness, have it checked by a clinician first. A hearing test is the usual starting point.[9]
  • Low-level background sound at bedtime or in quiet rooms is a sensible, low-risk comfort measure for many people. Keep volume comfortable, never loud enough to risk hearing damage, and stop if it makes tinnitus worse.
  • If tinnitus bothers you, the best-supported approaches target distress: cognitive behavioural therapy (recommended in both the 2026 U.S. and 2019 European guidelines) and a hearing-aid evaluation for people with hearing difficulties (recommended in the 2026 U.S. guideline).[9, 16]
  • Be cautious of any recording promising a cure, a “universal” frequency, or results in minutes, and of anything that asks you to pay for it.
