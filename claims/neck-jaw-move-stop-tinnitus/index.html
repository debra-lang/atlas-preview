Can a 30-Second Neck or Jaw Move Stop Tinnitus?

Tinnitus Claims: Fact vs. Evidence

! Misleading Evidence verdict

For some people tinnitus really does change with neck or jaw movement. A quick move that stops it has not been shown to work, and neck manipulation carries its own risks.

Evidence through 15 October 2026 · Last reviewed 15 October 2026 · What the verdicts mean · “Unsupported” does not necessarily mean disproven.

⚠ Safety note

Safety first. Do not twist, crack, hold or force your neck to try to stop tinnitus.

Call emergency services right away (911 in the U.S., 999 in the UK) for any sudden sign of stroke: numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg (especially on one side of the body, but any one sign is enough); confusion or trouble speaking; trouble seeing; trouble walking, dizziness or loss of balance; or a severe headache with no known cause.[1, 2]

Get urgent medical assessment for a new or unusual headache, new neck pain that is unusual for you, or tinnitus that beats in time with your pulse, with or without a neck injury or manipulation. If any of these comes with a stroke sign, call emergency services instead. This is our advice: the AHA/ASA statement lists these as possible signs of a torn neck artery (cervical artery dissection) but gives no timeframe.[3]

Why the caution: an AHA/ASA statement finds that most population studies link practitioner neck manipulation with stroke from a torn neck artery, but the evidence is not enough to show that manipulation causes it. Serious problems have also been reported after some gentler neck procedures. Reports do not prove cause.[3, 4]

The claim

“One quick neck, jaw or pressure-point move can silence the ringing in about 30 seconds.”

The short answer

There is a real kernel here. In some people, tinnitus gets louder, quieter or changes pitch when they clench the jaw, turn the head or press on certain muscles. Clinicians call this somatosensory (or somatic) tinnitus, because signals from the neck, jaw and face are interacting with the hearing system.

  • In a study of 121 tinnitus patients, 65% could change their tinnitus with muscle contractions of the head, neck or limbs; only 14% of 100 healthy volunteers could start a sound that way.[5]
  • But changing in the moment is not the same as stopping. In a clinic study of 81 patients, neck movements made tinnitus quieter in 30% and louder in 27%, and being able to modulate it did not predict a better outcome.[6]
  • Structured physiotherapy, jaw treatment or manual therapy, delivered over weeks by a trained clinician after assessment, helped some selected patients (people with tinnitus plus neck or jaw problems) in small trials. That is a treatment programme, not a 30-second trick.[7, 8, 9, 10]

What the research shows

What somatosensory tinnitus is, and how common it may be

The brain’s hearing pathways receive input from the neck, jaw and facial nerves. In some people that input appears to feed into tinnitus. Experts only agreed international diagnostic criteria for it in 2018, after finding that differing definitions produced very different prevalence figures between studies.[11] One U.S. study of 304 veterans with tinnitus estimated that 56% (90% interval 45% to 65%) had a somatosensory component, using head, neck and jaw maneuvers as the screen.[12] Having the feature does not mean the maneuver is a treatment: it is a clue that a neck or jaw problem may be worth examining.

Why a quick move does not equal a treatment

In the clinic study of 81 patients, 62% showed some change with a neck or jaw maneuver. Neck maneuvers reduced loudness in about 30% and made it worse in about 27%; jaw maneuvers suppressed tinnitus in about 24%. Being able to modulate tinnitus, or being treated for it, was not related to improvement afterwards.[6] A brief change while a muscle is tensed says little about what happens when the muscle is released a minute later, and the studies of quick maneuvers we found measured changes during the maneuver, not lasting relief.

What structured treatment trials found

Several randomised trials tested treatment programmes, not quick moves:

  • Neck physiotherapy. In 38 people with tinnitus and neck complaints, 12 sessions over six weeks lowered tinnitus questionnaire scores. 53% reported substantial improvement right after treatment, and 24% still did six weeks later. The authors said larger studies were needed.[7]
  • Jaw (orofacial) treatment. In 80 people with jaw-related tinnitus, treatment with physiotherapy and, where needed, a bite splint, produced a larger clinically relevant improvement on one questionnaire (Tinnitus Functional Index) than delayed treatment; another questionnaire showed no clinically relevant reduction.[8]
  • Manual therapy. In 61 people with jaw-related tinnitus, adding hands-on neck and jaw therapy to exercise and education gave better tinnitus-severity and handicap scores than exercise and education alone, assessed by a blinded examiner.[9]
  • Exercise, motion and stretching programme. In a 2023 randomised delayed-start pilot trial of 64 people with somatosensory tinnitus, at one hospital, three weeks of strengthening, joint-motion and stretching exercises lowered handicap (THI) and visual-analogue scores more than waiting; the groups no longer differed once the waiting group had been treated, and the effect was stable to 12 weeks.[13]
  • Trigger-point treatment. In 71 people with muscle pain and tinnitus, ten sessions of real trigger-point deactivation gave more tinnitus relief than sham treatment in a double-blind trial.[10]

Reviews are cautious. A 2016 review of six studies called the risk of bias high.[14] A 2023 review of four trials in jaw-related tinnitus found that physiotherapy reduced tinnitus intensity or severity, with low certainty.[15]

What the guidelines say

The two guidelines say different things, and neither recommends a self-applied quick move.

  • The 2026 U.S. guideline says clinicians may ask about somatosensory features (for example, whether tinnitus changes with jaw or neck movement) in people with persistent, bothersome tinnitus. That is a statement about taking a history. None of the key action statements listed in the abstract of its executive summary recommends physiotherapy, manual therapy or neck manipulation; the full text, which we did not review, may say more.[16]
  • The German guideline goes further and recommends offering manual and physiotherapeutic treatment when examination shows that tinnitus is modulated by findings in the neck or jaw, while noting the low methodological rigour and heterogeneity of the trials behind it.[17]

So the studies above evaluate treatment programmes in selected patients, and the U.S. guideline’s “ask about it” option should not be read as endorsing physiotherapy or neck manipulation.

Limitations and conflicts of interest

Trials in this area are small (38 to 80 people), usually cannot blind participants, often use a delayed-start comparison rather than a sham, and use different treatments and questionnaires, so they are hard to combine. Reviews rate the risk of bias as high or the certainty as low. Follow-up is short. Diagnostic criteria for somatosensory tinnitus were only agreed in 2018, so older studies defined it differently. Studies of the “instant” maneuvers shown in videos are essentially absent. We reviewed only the published abstract of the executive summary of the 2026 American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO-HNSF) tinnitus guideline, which lists its key action statements and scope; we did not review the executive summary’s full text or the full guideline, so statements here about what that guideline says are limited to those key action statements and that scope.

Tinnitus Evidence is independently operated, takes no advertising or affiliate payments, and sells no treatment. Several of the videos described above were posted by clinics or practitioners who offer neck, spine or jaw services, which gives them a commercial interest in the claim. Some of the trials cited were run by research groups that also deliver the treatment studied; each study page on this site records funding where it is reported.

What you can do

  • Notice whether jaw clenching, neck turning or pressing near the ear changes your tinnitus, and tell your clinician. Gentle observation is low-risk; forceful pulling, twisting or cracking is not.
  • Ask for a hearing test and an appropriate medical assessment, including a physical examination.[16] If neck or jaw findings are present, a physiotherapist or dentist experienced in jaw and neck disorders can design a programme for you, and teach exercises that are safe for your neck.
  • If jaw clenching or teeth grinding is part of the picture, a dentist can assess it and discuss options such as a bite splint.[8]
  • Whatever the cause, approaches that reduce how much tinnitus bothers you, such as cognitive behavioural therapy and, with hearing difficulties, a hearing-aid assessment, remain the best-supported options.[16]
