Do Vitamins, Herbs, or “Ear Formula” Supplements Work for Tinnitus?

Tinnitus Claims: Fact vs. Evidence

✕ Unsupported Evidence verdict

No supplement has been shown, in repeated good-quality trials, to relieve tinnitus. Some were tested without benefit; many marketed formulas were never tested. Unsupported is not the same as disproven.

The claim

“A natural vitamin, herb or ear formula can repair your ears or brain and stop the ringing, and doctors recommend it.”

The short answer

Supplements are among the most heavily advertised tinnitus products, and the evidence behind them is thin.

  • Guidelines: the 2026 U.S. guideline says clinicians “should not recommend dietary supplements for the primary indication of persistent, bothersome tinnitus”.[1] The 2019 European guideline recommends against them[2] and the German guideline says they “are to be omitted”.[3]
  • Tested, no benefit shown: ginkgo, zinc and vitamin B12 were each compared with placebo and did not show a reliable benefit.[4, 5, 6]
  • Inadequately tested: magnesium (one small, unpublished placebo-controlled result that showed no difference, plus an open-label study),[7] bioflavonoid formulas and most branded “ear formulas” have little or no adequate placebo-controlled evidence.
  • One exception worth knowing: a single manufacturer-funded trial of one multi-ingredient formula beat placebo on a handicap questionnaire; it has not been independently repeated.[8]

“Unsupported” here covers two different situations, and the table below keeps them apart: tested without benefit and not adequately tested.

Reviews that pool the evidence

A 2026 systematic review of nine randomised trials (390 adults) of over-the-counter oral treatments, including melatonin, ginkgo, alpha-lipoic acid, açaí and combination formulas, concluded that findings were “inconsistent and limited”, placebo responses were common, most products were studied in only one small trial, and no firm conclusions could be drawn about whether any provides benefit.[16] A 2025 network meta-analysis of 60 drug and supplement trials ranked ginkgo with vitamins first for tinnitus severity, but the certainty of that evidence was very low and the analysis found several inconsistent comparisons, so the authors described such antioxidants only as “promising” and called for better trials.[17]

Observational surveys can mislead too. A U.S. national survey linked low blood manganese, but not low zinc or vitamin B12, with more frequent and more disruptive tinnitus. The authors read this as a reason for further research, not as proof that taking a supplement helps.[18]

What regulators do and do not check

In the United States, supplement makers may describe an effect on the body’s structure or function without FDA pre-approval; these claims must carry a notice that FDA has not evaluated them and that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease.[19] A 2019 survey of over-the-counter tinnitus remedies noted that some brands advertise in ear, nose and throat journals and prominently feature supposed endorsement by ENT doctors; the authors described the remedies as unproven.[15]

A documented example: in 2016 the advertising industry’s National Advertising Review Board recommended that the maker of one bioflavonoid supplement stop claims that it eases or quiets the ringing. It found support only for the narrower statement that the product “may provide relief for some people who suffer from tinnitus”. A recommendation to modify advertising is not a finding of wrongdoing.[14]

Safety: “natural” does not mean risk-free

The 2019 European guideline says dietary and alternative therapies “pose potential harm” as well as lacking proven efficacy.[2] The German guideline notes that ginkgo can interact with blood thinners and increase bleeding risk in people with clotting problems.[3] The Cochrane review found little to no difference in bleeding or seizure risk in trials, with low certainty.[4] Ask a pharmacist or doctor before combining any supplement with other medicines.

CureGone and stays goneNo supplement has been shown to cure tinnitus.

Limitations and conflicts of interest

Most supplement trials are small and short, use different doses, formulas and questionnaires, and some report only before-and-after changes without a placebo group. Some have been run or funded by the product’s maker. For the individual ingredients above, “no benefit shown” means that the best available trials did not detect one, which is not proof that no individual could ever benefit. Several widely sold formulas have never been tested in people with tinnitus, so no conclusion can be drawn about them either way. Some details on funding come from the study report itself and from our Emerging page records; we flag where they have not been independently confirmed. We reviewed only the published abstract of the executive summary of the 2026 American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO-HNSF) tinnitus guideline, which lists its key action statements and scope; we did not review the executive summary’s full text or the full guideline, so statements here about what that guideline says are limited to those key action statements and that scope.

What you can do

  • Before spending money, ask what the label claims, whether a trial of that exact product in people with tinnitus exists, and who paid for it. “Doctor recommended” and “clinically proven” need a named study.
  • Do not stop prescribed medicines for a supplement. Tell your doctor or pharmacist what you take, especially ginkgo with blood thinners.[3]
  • If a blood test shows a deficiency of a vitamin or mineral, treating it is a separate medical decision, not a tinnitus treatment.
  • If tinnitus bothers you, the better-supported options reduce distress: cognitive behavioural therapy and, with hearing difficulties, hearing aids.[1]
  • If someone endorses a product with a celebrity or a “Harvard discovery”, check the claim on a fact-checking site before paying.[9]
