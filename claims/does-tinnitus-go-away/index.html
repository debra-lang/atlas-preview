Does Tinnitus Ever Go Away, or Is It Permanent?

Tinnitus Claims: Fact vs. Evidence

! Misleading Evidence verdict

Both blanket claims mislead. Most tinnitus that lasts for months tends to persist, some does fade or disappear, and how much it bothers you can often be reduced.

Evidence through 15 October 2026 · Last reviewed 15 October 2026 · What the verdicts mean · “Unsupported” does not necessarily mean disproven.

⚠ Safety note

Important safety note: if your hearing suddenly gets worse, get medical help right away.

Call emergency services right away (911 in the U.S., 999 in the UK) if tinnitus comes with facial weakness, sudden severe vertigo (a spinning sensation), or any sudden sign of stroke: numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg (especially on one side of the body, but any one sign is enough), confusion or trouble speaking, trouble seeing, trouble walking, dizziness or loss of balance, or a severe headache with no known cause.[1, 2, 3]

Go to an emergency department, or call emergency services, if tinnitus starts after a head injury.[3]

Get a medical evaluation right away if your hearing suddenly drops or seems muffled (all at once or over a few days), in one ear or both, with or without ringing. Do this even if it began several days ago. Some treatments are time-limited, but that is a reason to be seen now, not permission to wait. If you cannot be seen right away, go to an emergency department.[4, 2, 3, 5, 6]

Ask for an urgent appointment if your tinnitus beats in time with your pulse. This is UK NHS advice, not a U.S. rule. If it comes with any sign above, call emergency services.[3, 7]

If tinnitus has you thinking about harming yourself, seek urgent help now: in the US call or text 988; elsewhere use your local emergency or crisis line (see crisis support). Clinical guidelines tell clinicians to ask about self-harm in people with bothersome tinnitus.[7]

The claim

“Tinnitus is permanent and nothing helps,” or the opposite: “it will go away on its own if you wait.”

The short answer

“It’s permanent” and “it will go away” each contain some truth, and each misleads when stated as a rule.

  • Very brief ringing is treated differently. The 2026 U.S. guideline excludes “transient ear noise”, a sudden ringing in one ear lasting under 5 minutes, because it does not typically need clinical management.[7]
  • Established tinnitus tends to persist. In a five-year study of older adults, tinnitus persisted in 82% of those who had it at the start.[8] In a UK study of adults followed for four years, 18% of those who reported tinnitus reported none at follow-up; of those who still had it, 9% were less bothered, 9% were more bothered and the rest were unchanged.[9]
  • How much it bothers you is different from whether it is there. Cognitive behavioural therapy and, for people with hearing difficulties, a hearing-aid evaluation are recommended in the 2026 U.S. guideline for persistent, bothersome tinnitus. In studies they mainly reduce distress and daily impact; the sound often remains.[7]

No treatment has been shown to cure tinnitus, so “nothing can be done” is also wrong: a lot can be done about its impact.

What the research shows

What long-term studies show

Two population studies followed people over several years.

  • Blue Mountains Hearing Study (Australia, older adults): over five years, tinnitus present at the start was still present in 81.6%. Among those with tinnitus that was only mildly annoying at the start, 39.6% became moderately annoying and 5.9% severely annoying by the follow-up. Hearing loss roughly doubled the odds of new tinnitus (odds ratio 2.13).[8]
  • UK Biobank (adults aged 40-69, about 4,700 retested after four years): 18.3% of those who had reported tinnitus reported none at retest; of those who still had it, 9% reported improvement and 9% said it had become more bothersome. The authors concluded that resolution is relatively uncommon, and that improvement and worsening are equally likely.[9]

These are surveys of self-reported tinnitus, so some “resolution” may be fluctuation, and neither study followed people treated with a particular therapy. For most people, established tinnitus does not simply disappear; for some, it fades or becomes much less noticeable.

Why “permanent” is also an overstatement

Two things get blurred: whether the sound is present, and whether it is a problem. A national guideline asks clinicians to distinguish people who are bothered by tinnitus from people who are not, and educate patients about its natural history and ways to manage it.[7] Many people live with tinnitus that does not trouble them. For those it does trouble, several approaches have evidence:

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy. A Cochrane review of 12 studies (966 people) found CBT may reduce the impact of tinnitus on quality of life compared with other active treatments (small average effect, low certainty), and no evidence yet on effects at 6 or 12 months.[10] The 2019 European guideline gives it a strong recommendation.[11]
  • Hearing aids. For people with hearing difficulties and persistent, bothersome tinnitus, the 2026 U.S. guideline recommends a hearing-aid evaluation[7] and NICE says to offer amplification devices if hearing loss affects communication.[2] A Cochrane review found the evidence on hearing aids for tinnitus limited.[12]
  • Combination treatment. In a 461-person multicentre trial, every arm improved by week 12 on the Tinnitus Handicap Inventory; CBT and hearing aids alone had large effects that combining treatments did not clearly add to.[13]

Improvement in these studies is mainly in distress and daily impact, not in the sound disappearing.

What this means for “cure” and “wait and see” claims

“It will go away on its own” can delay getting hearing checked or treated, and can hide a treatable cause such as sudden hearing loss. “It’s permanent and nothing helps” can discourage people from the approaches most likely to make life easier. A balanced message is that tinnitus often persists, sometimes fades, and its impact can usually be reduced.

Limitations and conflicts of interest

The long-term data come from two population cohorts that relied on self-report and used single questions about tinnitus; their populations (older Australians; UK adults aged 40-69) may not match yours, and attrition between visits could bias the figures. Neither tested treatments. Trials of CBT and hearing aids are heterogeneous and usually follow people for weeks to months. Our percentage figures are as reported by the studies, not our own estimates, and different studies define “tinnitus” and “bothersome” differently. We reviewed only the published abstract of the executive summary of the 2026 American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO-HNSF) tinnitus guideline, which lists its key action statements and scope; we did not review the executive summary’s full text or the full guideline, so statements here about what that guideline says are limited to those key action statements and that scope.

What you can do

  • If tinnitus is new, one-sided, or comes with dizziness or a gradual change in hearing, get it checked,[2] starting with a hearing test.[7] If it beats in time with your pulse, or your hearing or balance changed suddenly, follow the safety note above.
  • If it has lasted beyond a few weeks and bothers you, ask your doctor or audiologist about support: tinnitus education, cognitive behavioural therapy (including online programmes), and a hearing-aid assessment if your hearing has changed.[2]
  • Do not judge a treatment by whether the sound disappears. Ask whether sleep, concentration, mood and daily life improve.
  • Be wary of anyone promising to switch tinnitus off, or telling you it is hopeless. Both ignore what the evidence shows.
