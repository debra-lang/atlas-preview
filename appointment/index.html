Prepare for your appointment

A family doctor or an audiologist (a hearing specialist) is usually the first step.3, 7 A little preparation helps you get the most from a short appointment, and leave with a plan.

Step 1 · Before you go

Before you go

  1. Start with how it affects you, in one sentence. For example: "Tinnitus is stopping me sleeping, and I've had to cut back at work." Doctors often interrupt within seconds, so say the most important thing first.1, 17
  2. Say why you're coming now, especially if you've had it a while.6
  3. Bring notes. Fill in the notes sheet below, and list your medicines and supplements.18
  4. Choose your top 3 questions from the list below.18, 19
  5. Bring someone if you can. A second pair of ears helps you remember what was said.20

Sudden hearing loss, new pulsing tinnitus or tinnitus after a head injury shouldn't wait for a routine appointment. When to get help quickly

Who to see first

  • US: your primary care doctor, or an audiologist for a full hearing test.3, 7 On Medicare you can see an audiologist once a year without a referral for gradual hearing changes; other insurance varies.12
  • UK: your GP. They can check your ears and refer you for a free NHS hearing test.8, 9 Some areas let you refer yourself to NHS audiology.
  • Australia: your GP.15
  • Elsewhere: your family doctor.

In the US and thinking about over-the-counter hearing aids? See a doctor first if your tinnitus is only in one ear, or your hearing changed suddenly.13

Step 4 · What to expect

What to expect

Good tinnitus care usually includes:

  • Questions about your tinnitus and your health, and a look in your ears.1
  • A full hearing test.1, 6
  • Questions about how much it bothers you, your sleep and your mood.1, 6
  • An explanation of what tinnitus is and what can help, and a plan you agree on.1, 6
  • Hearing aids if you have hearing loss, or a cochlear implant assessment if it is severe.1
  • CBT, a talking therapy that helps you cope, if tinnitus keeps affecting your life. In the UK it's often offered online first.1, 6, 8
  • A follow-up to see how you're doing.1

Questions about your mood are normal

You may be asked how you're feeling, and whether you've had thoughts of harming yourself. This is routine for anyone bothered by tinnitus, and it's safe to answer honestly. It helps your clinician find the right support.1, 4 Struggling now? Support and crisis lines

What usually isn't needed

A scan. Most people with tinnitus don't need one. Scans are for specific signs, such as tinnitus in one ear only, tinnitus that pulses, or new symptoms like weakness or numbness.1, 2, 6

Medicines or supplements for the tinnitus itself. They aren't recommended, although medicines may still help with sleep, anxiety or low mood.1

If your tinnitus changes when you move your jaw, head or neck, mention it. Some people are referred to a physiotherapist or dentist to check for a jaw or neck problem; the evidence that this helps is limited.1, 3 Read more

What a hearing test is like

A hearing test is painless. It usually takes 15 minutes to an hour, and you often get the results the same day.9

  • Audiogram (the main hearing test): you wear headphones and press a button whenever you hear a beep, even a very faint one.9, 10
  • Speech test: the same idea, with words instead of beeps.9
  • Middle-ear check (tympanometry): a soft tip rests in your ear for a few seconds to see how well your eardrum moves. Not everyone needs it.6, 9
  • A short questionnaire about how tinnitus affects you.6

Tips for test day

  • If loud sounds bother you, say so before you start. You can ask them to begin quietly, and you can stop at any time.10, 11
  • If you can't tell the beeps from your tinnitus, tell the tester. They can use a different kind of tone.10
  • Ask for a copy of your results.

Some clinics also try to match the pitch and loudness of your tinnitus. UK guidance doesn't recommend this, so don't worry if it isn't done.6

Step 5 · Afterwards

Before you leave

  • Check you've understood: "So the next step is... is that right?" Saying it back helps you remember.21
  • Ask who will contact whom, how long it might take, and what should make you come back sooner.
  • Ask for a copy of your hearing test and any written information.6, 18 You have a right to see your health records.22
  • Afterwards, jot down what was found and what was suggested, while it's fresh.

If you're told "nothing can be done"

There's no cure yet, but there is help. Advice, hearing care and CBT help many people feel better, even when the sound stays the same.1, 2, 6 Tinnitus charities advise doctors not to tell patients that nothing can be done.16

You could say: "I understand there's no cure yet. I'd like help with how it's affecting my sleep and mood. Could I have a full hearing test, and talk about options like CBT or a referral to audiology?"

If it still bothers you after this first advice, UK guidance says you should be referred for specialist tinnitus care.6 You can also ask to see another doctor.14

When it shouldn't wait for an appointment

Get help sooner than a routine appointment if you have:6, 8

  • Get emergency help now: sudden weakness in the face, arm or leg, trouble speaking or seeing, severe spinning dizziness that won't settle, or tinnitus after a recent head injury.
  • Get medical help today: a sudden drop in hearing (even if it started days or a few weeks ago), or new tinnitus that beats with your pulse and came on suddenly.
  • Book an appointment soon: tinnitus in one ear only, pulsing tinnitus that has been there a while (ask for an urgent appointment), or hearing that is getting worse.

What the research says

  • Strong guideline advice Clinicians should find out how much tinnitus bothers you. If it does, they should ask about your mood and any thoughts of self-harm.1
  • Guideline advice Everyone with tinnitus should be offered a full hearing test. CBT is recommended if tinnitus keeps bothering you.1, 6
  • Strong guideline advice Most people with tinnitus don't need a scan unless there are specific warning signs.1, 6
  • Some evidence A list of questions can help people ask more and get more information. Bringing someone along is linked to longer visits and more information, with mixed results.19, 20
  • One study About 1 in 5 people seeking help for tinnitus or sound sensitivity found standard test sounds uncomfortable, so asking to start quietly is reasonable.11
  • Practical tip Leading with the impact and saying why you're coming now are practical tips, based on what guidelines ask clinicians to explore.6, 17
