New to tinnitus? Start here

Hearing a sound no one else can hear can be frightening, especially at first. You are not alone: about 1 in 7 adults has some tinnitus,1 and it is usually not a sign of anything serious.2

The few signs that need quick help

Most tinnitus can wait for a routine appointment. A few situations can't:2, 3

  • Get emergency help now: sudden weakness in the face, arm or leg, trouble speaking or seeing, severe spinning dizziness that won't settle, or tinnitus after a recent head injury.
  • Get medical help today: a sudden drop in hearing (even if it started days or a few weeks ago), or new tinnitus that beats with your pulse and came on suddenly.
  • Book an appointment soon: tinnitus in one ear only, pulsing tinnitus that has been there a while, or tinnitus that is affecting your sleep or mood.

What tinnitus is

Tinnitus means hearing a sound, such as ringing, buzzing, hissing or humming, that doesn't come from outside you. It is a symptom, not a disease.5

It can follow loud noise, or come with hearing loss, an ear infection or earwax. Some medicines can cause it, and sometimes there's no clear reason.4 Most people with tinnitus have some hearing loss, which a hearing test can show.5 Stress doesn't usually cause tinnitus, but it can make it more noticeable.2

A sudden ringing in one ear that fades within about 5 minutes is different. The main US tinnitus guideline doesn't cover it, because it doesn't usually need medical care.5

The first few weeks: what helps

  • Use sound, not silence. Quiet rooms make tinnitus stand out. Low background sound, such as a fan, the radio or nature sounds, gives your ears something else to hear.2
  • Protect your ears from loud noise, but not from everyday sound. Wear earplugs at concerts or with power tools. In one trial at a music festival, 12% of people wearing earplugs had new tinnitus afterwards, against 40% without.11 But try not to block out ordinary sounds. Wearing earplugs for long stretches can make everyday sounds seem too loud.12, 13, 14
  • Look after your sleep. Tinnitus and poor sleep often go together. Sleep and difficult nights has practical steps for tonight and the longer term.
  • Keep doing the things you enjoy. Absorbing activities give your attention somewhere else to go. Try not to spend long periods listening for the sound.2
  • Be careful with forums and videos. Talking with others can help you feel less alone, but some online spaces are full of worst-case stories and conflicting advice.15 If reading makes you more frightened, take a break.
  • If you take a medicine you think might be linked, don't stop it on your own. Ask the person who prescribed it.

If tinnitus is bothering you, or affecting your sleep or mood, you don't have to wait months to ask for help.3, 13

Will it go away? An honest answer

Some tinnitus goes away, especially soon after it starts, but often it doesn't. Even when the sound stays, it often becomes less of a problem, and there is help for that.

  • Soon after it starts: two small studies followed people who saw an ear specialist in the first month. About 1 to 2 in 10 had no tinnitus 6 months later.9, 10 People whose tinnitus faded quickly may never have seen a specialist, so the real chance may be higher.
  • Tinnitus that has been there a while: a large UK study followed adults aged 40 to 69. About 2 in 10 no longer had it 4 years later. Of those who still had it, most said it was about the same; about 1 in 10 said it was better and 1 in 10 worse.8
  • How much it bothers people: on average this eases a little over time, though it varies a lot from person to person.7 Talking therapies such as CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) can reduce its impact on daily life.6

We can't promise that it will fade, or that everyone gets used to it, and anyone who promises either is guessing. What we can say is that there are ways to manage tinnitus that help many people live well with it.3 The full evidence: does tinnitus go away?

Before you spend money

Tinnitus attracts a lot of marketing, and it is natural to want a quick fix. Leading guidelines advise against these as tinnitus treatments:

  • Dietary supplements, such as ginkgo, zinc or vitamin blends sold for tinnitus.5 The evidence
  • Betahistine, a medicine sometimes prescribed for tinnitus or dizziness.3
  • Hearing aids if your hearing test shows no hearing loss. They are a good option when there is hearing loss.3
  • Antidepressants, anti-seizure or anti-anxiety medicines as a routine tinnitus treatment. They may still be right for you for other reasons, such as low mood.5
  • rTMS (magnetic brain stimulation).5 The evidence

Expensive sound gadgets aren't proven to work better than a fan, a radio or a free app. The UK guideline found too little evidence to recommend any particular sound therapy.3 If something promises a cure, check the claim or look it up in Emerging and unproven treatments.

Seeing a doctor about it

A family doctor (GP) or an audiologist is usually the first step. It helps to say clearly what is happening. For example:

  • "It started on [date], in [one / both] ears."
  • "It does / doesn't beat in time with my pulse."
  • "My hearing has / hasn't changed, and I do / don't feel dizzy."
  • "It's affecting my sleep / mood / work."
  • "Could I have a hearing test?"

If you're told nothing can be done, that usually means there's no cure, not that nothing can help. It's fine to ask about support for sleep and distress, or for a referral or second opinion.13 Prepare for your appointment has a notes sheet you can fill in and print.

What the research says

  • Guideline advice A hearing test (audiological assessment) is recommended for people with tinnitus.3, 5
  • Guideline advice Talking therapy based on CBT is recommended for tinnitus that keeps bothering you. It reduces the impact on daily life, not the sound itself; the evidence is rated low certainty.5, 6
  • Guideline advice Supplements, betahistine and rTMS are advised against as tinnitus treatments.3, 5
  • One trial Earplugs at a loud music festival reduced new tinnitus afterwards (12% vs 40%).11
  • Mixed advice Background sound is an option in the US guideline, but the UK guideline found too little evidence to recommend it. Many people find it comforting.2, 3, 5
  • Practical tip Keeping busy, limiting time on forums, and not over-using earplugs are expert advice with little direct research.12, 13, 15
