Family and relationships

Tinnitus doesn't only affect the person who hears it. Partners and families often feel it too, and couples often don't talk about it.1, 3 A few simple conversations can make home life easier for everyone.

Three things to try this week

  1. Explain it in 30 seconds. For example: "I hear a sound no one else can hear. It isn't dangerous. On bad days it makes me tired and short-tempered, and that isn't about you. What helps most is ___."
  2. Say what helps. People with tinnitus often say they want others to take it seriously, face them when talking, and accept background sound at night.3, 4
  3. Agree some ground rules for talking about it: when to talk, how you'll say it's a bad day (a code word works), and what would help then.6

For partners and family: what helps

Usually helps

  • Listen and believe them. Tinnitus is real, even though you can't hear it.
  • Ask what would help, such as choosing a quieter or less echoey place to meet.6, 7
  • Keep doing things together, and gently encourage them not to give up things they enjoy.6
  • Be patient. Getting used to tinnitus often takes months.10
  • Go to an appointment with them if they'd like you to.3
  • Wear earplugs with them at loud events.6
  • Look after yourself too.6, 7

Usually doesn't help

  • Brushing it off, or telling them to "just ignore it".
  • Checking on it all day. Agree ground rules instead.6
  • Pushing remedies, supplements or adverts. Check a claim first
  • Taking bad moods personally, especially in the early months.7

This list is our summary of charity guidance and small studies. In one study, more dismissive or punishing responses from a spouse went with more tinnitus disability.5

Around the house

  • Bedtime sound you can both live with. A pillow speaker plays sound only for the person lying on it.6, 8 Some couples sleep apart on bad nights or for a while. There's no research either way, so talk it through kindly so neither of you feels pushed away. Sleep and difficult nights
  • TV: turn on subtitles. A TV listener lets one person choose their own volume while the TV plays for everyone else, and a TV streamer sends sound straight to compatible hearing aids.11
  • Talking: face each other, speak clearly without shouting, rephrase rather than repeat, and turn down background noise at meals.12
  • If you notice the TV creeping up or missed words, gently suggest a hearing check.13 Prepare for your appointment

Getting help can help the whole household

  • Talking therapy. CBT, the best-supported help for tinnitus distress, can make tinnitus easier to live with. In one early study, family members felt less burdened after the person did online CBT, although it had no comparison group.2 CBT: the evidence
  • Support groups often welcome family and friends.6 Support organizations
  • Counselling together can help if tensions are hard to manage.6

Children and teenagers

Explaining a parent's tinnitus (our suggestion): "Sometimes my ears make a noise only I can hear. It isn't dangerous, and it's nobody's fault. Some days it makes me tired, and that isn't about you."

If your child has tinnitus: it's common in children, most aren't troubled by it, and a simple explanation and some reassurance is usually enough. Ask them about it gently.9 See a doctor if they're distressed, or have ear pain, a blocked feeling, dizziness or hearing loss. Tinnitus that beats with the pulse should always be checked.9

If you're worried about their mood or safety

If you think they might be thinking about suicide, it's OK to ask them directly and kindly. Asking doesn't put the idea in their head; it gives them a chance to talk.14, 15

  • Listen, and take what they say seriously. You don't have to fix it.14
  • Help them connect with support: a crisis line, their doctor, or someone they trust.15 Crisis lines by country
  • If they are in immediate danger, call your emergency number and don't leave them alone.15
  • Keep in touch afterwards.15

Supporting someone can be hard. Look after yourself too, and you can call a crisis line for advice about someone else.14 When it feels like too much is written for the person with tinnitus.

What the research says

  • Small studies Most partners say tinnitus affects them too, and couples often don't talk about it.1, 3
  • Association Dismissive or punishing responses from a spouse went with more tinnitus disability; this can't show cause.5
  • Early evidence Family members felt less burdened after the person did online CBT, in a study without a comparison group.2
  • Official advice Asking someone directly about suicide doesn't increase risk and can help them open up.14, 15
  • Practical tip The 30-second explanation, ground rules and household plan are practical tools from charity guidance; they haven't been tested as such.6

Research on tinnitus and families is small, so most of this page is practical guidance from tinnitus and hearing charities, labelled as such.
