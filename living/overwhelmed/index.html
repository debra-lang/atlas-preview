When it feels like too much

Living with a sound you can't switch off can be exhausting. If it feels like too much right now, you're not alone, and with the right help it can get easier.

If you're struggling right now

  1. If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

    If you're thinking about suicide, or feel you can't keep yourself safe, please talk to someone now. The lines below are free and confidential. Crisis lines
  2. Then, one small step at a time
  3. 2
    Take a few slow breaths, or try 5 to 10 minutes of relaxation. Relaxation helped people in a tinnitus trial.6, 10
  4. 3
    Give your attention somewhere gentle to go: a sound you like, a short walk, or something that absorbs you.6
  5. 4

  6. 5

When you're ready

What you may be feeling

Many people with tinnitus feel anxious, low, angry or hopeless at times, especially when sleep suffers. In studies of people seeking help for tinnitus, around 1 in 3 had depression, and anxiety is common too.1, 2

Feeling this way doesn't mean you've failed to cope. It means you need, and deserve, support.

Small steps that help

  • Plan one small, enjoyable thing each day, and gently return to things you've stopped doing because of tinnitus. Planning pleasant activities is a recommended way to ease low mood.13, 14
  • Keep doing what matters to you, with short breaks rather than pushing through.
  • Move your body. Gentle exercise, especially with others, can help with low mood.13
  • Practise relaxation, such as slow breathing or relaxing your muscles one by one.6, 10
  • Look after your sleep. Everything feels harder after bad nights. Sleep and difficult nights
  • Take breaks from forums and from searching for cures. Some people leave online forums feeling there is no help, which isn't true.12
  • Connect with people who understand. Many people say tinnitus support groups help them feel less alone and more hopeful.6, 11 Support organizations

Help that works: talking therapies

CBT is the best-supported help for tinnitus distress, and US, UK and European guidelines recommend it.3, 4, 5 CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) is a talking therapy. It helps you change the thoughts and habits that make tinnitus harder to live with. On average it reduces how much tinnitus affects daily life and mood, though it usually doesn't change the sound itself.3

Online CBT with some support from a therapist or audiologist also helps many people. In one trial it worked about as well as group CBT.7, 8 Two related therapies, acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) and mindfulness-based therapy (MBCT), have also helped in smaller trials.9, 10

How to get CBT-based help where you live

  • UK: ask your GP. You'll usually be offered online CBT first.5, 6 In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for anxiety or low mood, including when you have a long-term condition. It's for adults, and it's general support, not tinnitus-specific.15 Is distress stopping you doing your usual daily activities, even after first support? Then NICE says you should be referred and seen within 2 weeks. You can ask your GP about this.5
  • US: ask your doctor or an audiologist about CBT-based tinnitus care. The American Tinnitus Association's directory lists local providers, but it doesn't check them. Ask whether they offer CBT-based tinnitus care.16 The ABCT finder lists CBT therapists (top listings are paid).17 Veterans can ask VA audiology about Progressive Tinnitus Management.18
  • Australia: if tinnitus has led to depression or anxiety, your usual GP can make a Mental Health Treatment Plan. Medicare then covers up to 10 individual and 10 group sessions a year.19 MindSpot offers free online courses.20
  • Canada: in Ontario, Ontario Structured Psychotherapy offers free CBT for depression and anxiety.21 Elsewhere, ask your family doctor.
  • Germany: Kalmeda is a tinnitus CBT app in the official DiGA directory. A doctor can prescribe it, and statutory health insurance pays.22

Free places to start

  • The Living Better with Tinnitus workbook (US VA and Department of Defense), free for anyone, covering sound, pleasant activities, relaxation and balancing thoughts.14
  • Tinnitus UK's free Take on Tinnitus online course. It hasn't been formally evaluated.23

Before you spend money

When you feel desperate, offers of a cure can be very tempting. Before you pay for anything, ask:

  1. Has it been tested against a placebo or sham treatment in people with tinnitus? Check a claim
  2. Do guidelines advise against it? Supplements, rTMS, betahistine, and medicines used to treat the tinnitus itself are advised against.4, 5
  3. Are there warning signs? "Miracle cure", "all natural", one product that does it all, quick fixes, glowing testimonials, or claims that doctors are hiding it.24
  4. Is there a big upfront cost or subscription with no trial or refund?
  5. What does your doctor or audiologist think? Free, guideline-backed help (talking therapy, sound, hearing aids if you have hearing loss) comes first.4, 24

When to get more help

If tinnitus is affecting your mood, sleep or work most days, tell your doctor. You can say: "It's affecting my mental health." You may be asked about your mood and whether you've had thoughts of harming yourself. That's routine, and it's safe to answer honestly.4 Prepare for your appointment

What the research says

  • Best-supported CBT is recommended by US, UK and European guidelines for tinnitus that keeps bothering you. The evidence is low certainty, and most studies follow people only to the end of treatment.3, 4, 5
  • Moderate benefit Online CBT with support from a therapist or audiologist helps many people, though many studies had high drop-out.7, 8
  • Smaller trials ACT and mindfulness-based therapy (MBCT) helped in single trials of 64 and 75 people.9, 10
  • Guideline advice Planning activities and group exercise are recommended for less severe depression in general.13
  • People's experience Many people describe support groups as helping them feel connected and hopeful; this hasn't been measured as an outcome.11
  • Practical tip Taking breaks from forums, pacing yourself and the support plan are expert advice with little direct research.12

We don't promise that tinnitus will go away. We point to the help with the best evidence for making it easier to live with.
