Spikes and difficult days

Tinnitus often goes up and down: most people who seek help say theirs varies.1 A louder spell can be frightening, but it often eases back towards your usual level.

If it's louder today

  1. Do a quick check. Has your hearing suddenly dropped? Is there new dizziness, weakness in your face, or a recent head injury? Is it newly beating with your pulse? If yes, get checked today. If not, this sounds like an ordinary spike.12
  2. Add gentle sound. Quiet rooms are the most common thing people say makes tinnitus worse.7 A fan, music or the radio at a low level can take the edge off.12
  3. Slow your breathing, or try a short relaxation exercise. Stress and tinnitus often rise and fall together.2, 12
  4. Give your attention somewhere else: a walk, a phone call, a task that absorbs you. Try not to keep checking whether it's still there.12, 18
  5. Keep your plans, at a gentler pace. Avoiding things can make tinnitus feel more important.18, 19

Will it settle?

For most people, tinnitus goes up and down. A louder spell often eases back towards your usual level, sometimes within hours, sometimes over a few days.2, 3 Researchers haven't yet measured exactly how long spikes last, and they can be hard to predict.4

It's also common for tinnitus to seem louder at night or first thing in the morning.5 And a lasting change is less common than you might fear: in a large UK study, most people said their tinnitus bothered them about the same amount 4 years later.6

A few words to tell yourself

"This is a spike. I've had louder days before, and they've eased. A louder day doesn't usually mean something is wrong. I don't need to keep checking it. I'll put on some gentle sound and do one small thing I enjoy."

Noticing a worried thought and answering it kindly is a technique from CBT.20

What people often notice before a spike

What goes with louder days differs from person to person.3, 7 Things people often mention:

  • Quiet rooms, and bedtime.7
  • Stress or worry.2, 7
  • A poor night's sleep.7 Help for difficult nights
  • Loud noise, such as a concert or power tools.7
  • A cold or other illness, or ears blocked with wax.8, 14
  • A new medicine. Ask your pharmacist or doctor, and don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own.

What about coffee? Studies haven't found that caffeine makes tinnitus worse.9, 10 Stopping suddenly can cause headaches, so keep your usual amount steady. It can still affect sleep, so go easy in the evening.21

And alcohol? It affects people differently: in one study, most noticed no change.11

After a loud event

Ringing after a concert or other loud noise usually fades by the next day.16 Dull or muffled hearing after loud noise often recovers within a day or two.15 If your hearing still feels reduced after that, or drops suddenly, get it checked the same day. Even when it settles, it's a sign your ears needed protecting.15

Carry earplugs for loud places. But don't wear them in everyday quiet, because that can make you more sensitive to sound.13

When a change needs checking

Most spikes aren't a sign of anything serious. Get help if a change comes with any of these:12, 17

  • Get emergency help now: tinnitus after a head injury, or with weakness in your face, arm or leg, trouble speaking, or severe spinning dizziness that won't settle.
  • Get medical help today: a sudden drop in hearing, hearing that hasn't recovered a day or two after loud noise, or new tinnitus that beats with your pulse.
  • Book an appointment soon: a spike that keeps getting worse or hasn't started to ease after about a week, a change in one ear only, or tinnitus that is affecting your sleep or mood.

If spikes keep coming

  • Try a simple spike diary for 2 to 4 weeks, filled in once a day, then stop. Note how intrusive it was (0 to 10), how you slept, stress, loud noise, illness and any new medicine. Look for your own pattern, and test one suspected trigger at a time.21 Keep it short: in one study, half of people noticed their tinnitus more while tracking it.2
  • See your doctor for a check-up and a hearing test if spikes are frequent and you can't work out why.12
  • Get longer-term help. CBT, a talking therapy, can reduce how much tinnitus affects your life.22 CBT: the evidence

What the research says

  • Consistent studies Tinnitus loudness and bother go up and down within the same person, and tend to move together with stress.1, 2, 3
  • Not yet measured How long spikes last, and how often they return to baseline, hasn't been studied.4
  • Trial evidence Cutting out caffeine didn't help tinnitus in a blinded trial, and caused withdrawal symptoms.9
  • NHS advice Avoid total silence, try relaxation, don't focus on the sound, and don't wear earplugs all the time.12, 13
  • Low certainty CBT may reduce the impact of tinnitus on daily life.22
  • Practical tip The quick check, the words to tell yourself, the spike plan and the diary are practical tools built from expert advice; they haven't been tested as such.18, 20, 21

Most of what we know about spikes comes from people rating their tinnitus on their phones through the day. It shows patterns, not causes.
