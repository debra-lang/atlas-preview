Sound sensitivity and hearing protection

Many people with tinnitus also find some everyday sounds too loud or uncomfortable.1 It's tempting to block everything out, but over-protecting your ears can make sensitivity worse.2 The aim is to protect your ears from truly loud sound, and gently get used to everyday sound again.

Things to try today

  1. Keep earplugs for genuinely loud things, such as power tools, concerts and motorbikes. A simple test: if you'd have to shout to be heard by someone an arm's length away, protect your ears.5
  2. Leave them out for everyday sounds like the kitchen, the supermarket or traffic. Wearing earplugs all the time can make you more sensitive to sound.2, 3
  3. Have gentle sound around you, such as quiet music or a fan, rather than silence.2
  4. Remember: uncomfortable isn't the same as harmful. Sounds below about 70 dB, like normal conversation, are unlikely to damage hearing, even over long periods.9
  5. Step away rather than plugging up when a sound is too much, and come back when you're ready.

What's going on?

Hyperacusis means everyday sounds seem much louder than they should, or even painful.2 Misophonia is different: strong anger, disgust or anxiety at particular sounds, often other people chewing or breathing, whatever their volume.4 The two can overlap, and both can come with tinnitus.3

In a large Swedish study, more than half of people with tinnitus said some sounds were too loud or uncomfortable. Only about 1 in 10 had a severe problem.1

Protect, or don't plug?

Protect your ears

Power tools, mowers and leaf blowers; concerts and festivals; motorbikes; fireworks; shooting; loud workplaces.8

Carry earplugs, and use them if you'd have to shout

Cinemas, gym classes, loud bars, sports events.5, 8

Leave them out

The kitchen, the supermarket, traffic, conversation, the office.2, 3

Wearing foam earplugs properly

  1. With clean hands, roll the earplug into a thin, crease-free "snake".
  2. Reach over your head and pull the top of your ear up and back.
  3. Slide the earplug in and hold it for 20 to 30 seconds while it expands.
  4. Check: your own voice should sound muffled. If cupping your hands over your ears makes things much quieter, the fit isn't right, so try again.
  5. To remove, twist it slowly.

This is the NIOSH "roll, pull, hold" method.6, 8

Other types: reusable earplugs, including "high-fidelity" or musician's earplugs. These turn the volume down evenly, so they suit concerts and cinemas. Earmuffs are the easiest to fit. A hearing professional can also make custom earplugs. The best type is the one you'll actually wear.8

On your phone

  • iPhone: Settings, then Sounds & Haptics, then Headphone Safety. Turn on Reduce Loud Audio to lower headphone volume above a level you choose.10
  • Keep headphone volume below about 60% of maximum.11
  • Your weekly listening budget: about 80 dB for up to 40 hours a week, 90 dB for about 4 hours, and 100 dB for about 20 minutes.11
  • A free sound level meter app from NIOSH (iPhone) gives a rough reading. Use it to check now and then, not to monitor all day.7

Help that works

  • An audiologist can offer hyperacusis therapy: gradually getting used to sound again, often with sound therapy and advice. Evidence for sound therapy is limited, and services vary.3, 14
  • CBT that includes gradual sound exposure helped people with hyperacusis in a trial, compared with waiting for treatment.12 CBT: the evidence
  • For misophonia, group CBT helped in one good trial. Other approaches are mostly untested.13

There's no UK professional guideline for hyperacusis yet; the British Society of Audiology says it is working on one.15

What to tell your audiologist

  • Which sounds bother you, where, and how much. Is it the loudness, fear, anger or pain?
  • How many hours a day you wear earplugs or ear defenders.
  • What you're avoiding, and what you'd like to get back to.
  • "Please start the hearing test quietly, and let me stop if it hurts."16 Prepare for your appointment

A gradual sound plan to take to your audiologist

  1. List the everyday sounds you avoid or plug out.
  2. Put them in order, from easiest to hardest.
  3. Have low, steady background sound around you each day.
  4. Cut down earplug time step by step, starting with the easiest situations.
  5. Note your discomfort from 0 to 10, and how it changes over the weeks.
  6. Review it with your audiologist.

Based on what CBT and sound-therapy programmes use; the evidence is limited. Do this with an audiologist, not instead of one.12, 14

When to get checked quickly

Most sound sensitivity can wait for a routine appointment. Get help sooner for:18, 19, 20

  • Get emergency help now: weakness or drooping of the face with arm weakness or trouble speaking, sound sensitivity that started after a head injury, or severe spinning dizziness that won't settle.
  • Get medical help today: severe ear pain with a rash on or in the ear, ear pain with fluid coming out, swelling around the ear or a high temperature, earache lasting more than 2 to 3 days, or a sudden drop in hearing.
  • Book an appointment soon: everyday sounds that feel too loud or painful most of the time, or sound sensitivity that is stopping you doing things.

What the research says

  • One trial CBT with gradual sound exposure helped hyperacusis compared with a waiting list (60 people).12
  • One trial Group CBT helped misophonia compared with a waiting list (54 people).13
  • Limited evidence Sound therapy for hyperacusis is widely used, but the studies are few and conflicting.14
  • Official advice Don't wear earplugs all the time; protect your ears from genuinely loud sound; keep listening within weekly limits.2, 5, 11
  • Lab studies Short experiments in volunteers show that blocking an ear for days makes sounds seem louder, which is why over-protection may backfire.17
  • Practical tip The protect-or-not table, the gradual sound plan and the audiologist checklist are practical tools built from the advice above.3

We describe types of hearing protection, not brands. The best earplug is the one you'll wear when it matters.
