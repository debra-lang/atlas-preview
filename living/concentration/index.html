Concentration, work and study

If tinnitus makes it harder to focus, you're not imagining it: many people with tinnitus find concentration harder, and studies back this up.1, 2 Small, practical changes often help.

Things to try today

  • Your space

    • Use low, steady background sound while you work, such as a fan, gentle music or nature sounds, a little quieter than your tinnitus.6 Treat it as a tool you can use when it helps, not something you must always have on.5
    • Choose a quieter seat, away from printers, busy walkways or rattling air vents, if you can.9

  • Your day

    • Do your hardest task at your best time of day, and break long tasks into shorter chunks.
    • Take short, regular breaks. Trying to push through for hours tends to raise stress.7

  • Your energy

    • Look after your sleep. Tiredness makes focusing much harder. Help for difficult nights

If you strain to follow conversations, a hearing check is worth booking: hearing difficulties can drain concentration more than tinnitus itself.3, 4 Prepare for your appointment

At work

  • Background sound at your desk: a quiet fan or a low, pleasant sound through one earbud or a small speaker.6, 9
  • Meetings and video calls: turn on captions, sit where you can see faces, and ask for written notes afterwards.9
  • Noise-cancelling headphones can help you keep the volume low in noisy places.10 If you use them to block office chatter, play a soft sound through them. Total silence can make tinnitus more noticeable.
  • On a headset all day? Keep the volume below about 60% of maximum and give your ears breaks between calls.10

Asking for changes

Small adjustments are often easy for an employer: a quieter desk, a sound machine, flexible hours or home working, spread-out breaks, captions in meetings.9, 11 You don't have to tell anyone about your tinnitus unless you want to.11

You could say: "I have tinnitus, a constant sound in my ears. It can make concentrating harder in quiet rooms or long meetings. A few small changes help me, such as [a quieter desk with low background sound / captions in meetings / short breaks / some home working]. Could we try them for a month and review?" Keep a note of what you agree.

  • US: you can ask in plain words; you don't need to say "ADA" or "reasonable accommodation".15 Concentrating and hearing count as major life activities under the ADA.16 The free Job Accommodation Network lists ideas for tinnitus.9
  • UK: if tinnitus has a big, long-term effect on daily life, it can count as a disability under the Equality Act. Your employer must then make reasonable adjustments.11, 12 Acas has a free template letter for asking. Access to Work can fund extra support, but not the adjustments your employer must make by law.13 (Northern Ireland has different rules.)

At college or university

  • Contact disability or student support services early. Colleges and universities must make reasonable adjustments, and you usually need to ask.12
  • Ask what is available, such as recorded lectures, note-taking support, seating near the front, or a quieter room for exams.
  • In England, Disabled Students' Allowance can pay for equipment or support after a needs assessment.14
  • Study in short blocks with breaks, with low background sound rather than silence.6, 7

If your work is noisy

Did your tinnitus start or get worse with noise at work? Tell your employer, and ask about a noise assessment, hearing protection and hearing checks. Above certain noise levels these are legal duties.17, 18

  • UK: from 80 dB(A), your employer must assess the risk and give you hearing protection if you ask. From 85 dB(A), protection must be provided and worn. People who already have hearing loss may need regular hearing checks.17
  • US: from 85 dBA averaged over a workday, employers must provide free hearing protection and yearly hearing tests.18

For music and other listening, the World Health Organization's safe level for adults is about 80 dB for up to 40 hours a week. Louder sound means less time.10

Why focusing can feel harder

Studies find small-to-moderate links between tinnitus and three things: holding your attention, switching between tasks, and thinking quickly on hard tasks.1, 2 One explanation is that the sound competes for your attention. These are links, not proof that tinnitus causes the difficulty. Many people with tinnitus do just as well as anyone else.2

Other things often play a part, and most of them can be helped: hearing loss (listening takes more effort),3, 4 poor sleep, stress, anxiety and low mood.1

If it keeps affecting your work or study

  1. Get a hearing check. If you have hearing loss, even mild, hearing aids can make listening easier.8, 19 Hearing aids: the evidence
  2. Treat poor sleep. CBT for insomnia has the best evidence for long-term sleep problems.20 CBT-I: the evidence
  3. Consider CBT for tinnitus. It can reduce how much tinnitus interferes with daily life, and it's available online. Evidence on work and concentration specifically is limited.21, 22 CBT · Online CBT

What the research says

  • Consistent but small Reviews find small-to-moderate links between tinnitus and attention, task-switching and processing speed. They are associations, not proof of cause.1, 2
  • Small studies When hearing is taken into account, hearing difficulties can explain more of the struggle than tinnitus itself.3
  • Guideline advice Hearing aids are recommended when hearing loss affects communication, and CBT for tinnitus that keeps bothering you.19, 21
  • Official guidance Safe listening (about 80 dB for up to 40 hours a week) and noise-at-work duties come from the WHO and national regulators.10, 17, 18
  • Practical tip Background sound, breaks, quieter seating and the manager script are expert advice with little direct research. Trying to control every sound can backfire, so use sound flexibly.5, 6, 9

Most research on tinnitus and concentration uses lab tasks, not real workplaces, so practical tips here are labelled as such.
