Social life, restaurants and travel

Tinnitus can make noisy places and trips feel daunting, and many people start saying no to things they used to enjoy.1 With a little planning, most of them are still possible.

Before you go out

  1. Pick a calmer venue and time. Soft furnishings such as carpet, curtains and padded seats soak up noise, and off-peak times are quieter.1
  2. Ask for the right table: a booth or the quietest area, away from the bar, speakers and kitchen. If tinnitus is in one ear, put your better ear towards your companions.1
  3. Tell the people you're with. Tinnitus is invisible, and most people are understanding once they know.1
  4. Keep earplugs in your pocket for genuinely loud places. Leave them out at ordinary volumes, because over-protecting can make sound sensitivity worse.4, 5
  5. Struggling to follow conversation? Sit where you can see faces, and ask people to rephrase rather than repeat.6

Restaurants, parties and the cinema

  • Restaurants: it's fine to ask staff to turn down music that's too loud.1
  • Parties: move to a quieter room or outside, talk to one person at a time, and accept that you'll miss some of it.1
  • Cinema: films can be very loud. Take earplugs, or look for subtitled or relaxed screenings.1
  • Noise ratings: some apps and review sites show how loud venues are. Readings are rough and user-submitted, but they can help you choose.

Phone tools that can help

  • Live Listen turns an iPhone into a microphone that sends voices to your AirPods. Put the phone near the person you want to hear, and keep the volume moderate.7
  • Live Captions shows captions of speech around you on iPhone 11 or later, in some languages. Accuracy varies.8
  • If you have hearing loss, hearing aids with a remote microphone can make noisy places much easier.6 Hearing aids: the evidence

You could say to friends: "I have tinnitus, a constant sound in my ears. Noisy rooms make it hard to follow conversation. If I lean in or ask you to repeat, that's why. A quieter table really helps."

When booking: "Could we have a booth, or a table away from the speakers and kitchen? I have a hearing condition."

Concerts, festivals and loud events

  • Wear earplugs. Filtered earplugs turn the volume down without muffling the music.1 In a festival trial, earplugs cut new tinnitus after the event from 40% to 12%.9
  • Stay away from the speakers. Sound drops quickly with distance.4
  • Take a 10-minute quiet break every hour.4
  • Ringing afterwards often fades. If it doesn't, or your hearing still feels muffled after a day or two, get it checked.4, 10 When to get help quickly

Flying

Most people with tinnitus have no problems flying, and any effect is usually minor and temporary.2

  • Help your ears with the pressure: swallow, yawn, chew gum or suck a sweet, especially as you come in to land, and try to stay awake for the descent.2, 11 Pinching your nose and blowing very gently can help too; never force it.11
  • Try not to fly with a heavy cold. Ask a pharmacist about decongestants, which aren't suitable for everyone.2, 11
  • Keep your hearing aids in.2
  • Don't seal out all sound with earplugs, because it can make tinnitus more noticeable. If engine noise bothers you, sit in front of the wings.2 Noise-cancelling headphones are fine at a low volume with something pleasant playing.4
  • Pressure-regulating earplugs are widely sold, but there's little evidence they help.12
  • Had ear surgery? Check with your specialist before flying.2

Holidays and travel

  • Pack your bedtime sound: a phone app, a small speaker or a fan setting, so unfamiliar quiet rooms are easier.3
  • Jet lag: shift your sleep times gradually before you go, get daylight when you arrive, and avoid naps.13 Sleep and difficult nights
  • Airport security (US): you don't have to remove hearing aids or cochlear implants.14
  • Travel insurance: declare existing health conditions, and check with your insurer whether to list tinnitus.15
  • Diving: new tinnitus during or after a dive needs a prompt medical check. Ask a diving doctor before diving with existing tinnitus if you're unsure.16

Getting back to things you've stopped

Avoiding things brings quick relief, but over time it can keep worry about tinnitus going.17, 18 A gentle, step-by-step plan can help you get back to what matters:

  1. List what you've stopped doing because of tinnitus.
  2. Put them in order, from easiest to hardest.
  3. Start with a short, easy version, such as a quiet café mid-afternoon for 30 minutes.
  4. Stay until the worry eases a little, rather than leaving at the first discomfort.
  5. Afterwards, compare what happened with what you expected.
  6. Repeat, then move up a step.

What the research says

  • Consistent studies Avoidance and fear of tinnitus go with more distress and disability; CBT works partly by reducing them.17, 18
  • One trial Earplugs at a music festival reduced new tinnitus afterwards (12% vs 40%).9
  • Little evidence A systematic review found too little evidence that pressure-regulating earplugs prevent ear problems when flying.12
  • Official guidance Safe listening at loud events: distance from speakers, hourly breaks, earplugs.4
  • Practical tip Venue choice, seating, scripts, flying tips and the step-by-step plan are expert advice from tinnitus and hearing charities, with little direct research.1, 2, 6
