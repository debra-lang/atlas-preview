When to get help quickly

Most tinnitus is not a sign of anything dangerous. A few situations need medical attention quickly. This page explains which ones, calmly and without guesswork.

Emergency numbers: US and Canada: 911 · UK: 999 or 112 · Ireland and the EU: 112 (Ireland also 999) · Australia: 000 · New Zealand: 111.

Get emergency help now

Call your emergency number or go to an emergency department if tinnitus comes with:

  • sudden weakness or drooping of the face, arm or leg, numbness, trouble speaking or seeing, a sudden severe headache, or other sudden changes in how your body works. These can be signs of a stroke.
  • pulsing tinnitus together with new head or neck pain, especially after a neck injury or neck manipulation. This can be a sign of a torn artery in the neck.
  • sudden, severe spinning dizziness (vertigo) that does not settle.
  • a recent head injury.

Get medical help today

Contact a doctor the same day if:

  • your hearing has suddenly dropped or sounds muffled in one or both ears, even if it started days or a few weeks ago. Treatment works best when started early. If you cannot be seen today, go to an emergency department or urgent care.
  • you have new tinnitus that beats in time with your pulse and came on suddenly.
  • you have ear pain with fluid coming from the ear, swelling around the ear or a high temperature, or earache that has lasted more than 2 to 3 days.

Book an appointment soon

Arrange a non-urgent appointment with your doctor or an audiologist if:

  • you hear tinnitus in one ear only and it does not go away.
  • you have pulsing tinnitus that has been there for a while. The NHS advises asking for an urgent GP appointment for this.
  • your hearing is getting worse over weeks, or is clearly different between your ears.
  • you keep getting earache, or dizziness that keeps coming back.
  • tinnitus is affecting your sleep, mood, concentration or daily life.

If you feel you can't cope

If you are thinking about suicide, or feel you cannot cope, you can talk to someone now. If you are in immediate danger, call your emergency number. Crisis lines by country

Where this guidance comes from

  • NHS: Tinnitus (reviewed 12 Jan 2024; checked 26 Oct 2026): go to A&E or call 999 for tinnitus after a head injury, or with sudden hearing loss, facial weakness or vertigo; urgent GP appointment for tinnitus that beats with your pulse
  • NHS: Hearing loss (reviewed 30 May 2025): urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for sudden hearing loss
  • NICE NG155 (2020), recommendations 1.2.1 to 1.2.7, checked against nice.org.uk on 26 Oct 2026: refer immediately to a crisis mental health team for tinnitus with a high risk of suicide; refer immediately for sudden-onset significant neurological symptoms or signs, acute uncontrolled vertigo or suspected stroke; within 24 hours for hearing loss that developed suddenly (over 3 days or less) in the past 30 days; within 2 weeks for distress affecting mental wellbeing, or hearing loss that is rapidly worsening; consider referral for persistent pulsatile or one-sided tinnitus (our summary, not an official quotation)
  • Ellis et al., British Journal of General Practice 2022: sudden-onset pulsatile tinnitus warrants emergency assessment
  • NHS: Earache (reviewed 27 Oct 2025): urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for earache lasting more than 2 to 3 days, fluid from the ear, swelling around the ear, a very high temperature or a change in hearing
  • NIDCD: Sudden deafness (updated 14 Sep 2018): treat as a medical emergency; treatment delayed more than two to four weeks is less likely to help
  • RNID: Sudden hearing loss (updated 27 May 2026): can be a medical emergency; contact NHS 111 or your GP, or go to A&E if you cannot
  • CDC: Signs and Symptoms of Stroke (page dated 19 May 2026): call 911 right away for any sudden sign of stroke, including a severe headache with no known cause
  • Keser, Meschia and Lanzino, Mayo Clinic Proceedings 2022 (PMID 35379423), and Pacheco-López et al., Journal of Clinical Medicine 2025 (PMID 40648801) (both reviewed for Tinnitus Claims Check, Oct 2026): pulsatile tinnitus with head or neck pain can signal a torn neck artery, which needs emergency assessment
  • AAO-HNSF Clinical Practice Guideline: Tinnitus Update (Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery, Oct 2026; doi 10.1002/ohn.70417): clinicians should ask about self-harm risk (KAS 3) and obtain a comprehensive audiological evaluation (KAS 5)
